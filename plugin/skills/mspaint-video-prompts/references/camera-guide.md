# Camera and composition guide

Use this when deciding the CAMERA and POSITION lines for each image. The goal is a shot that makes the *line being spoken* more interesting and easier to understand, and a sequence that doesn't feel repetitive.

## Contents

2. Camera angles
3. Matching the shot to the line
4. Sequence rules

## 1. Shot sizes

| Shot | What it shows | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Extreme close-up | One small detail (an eye, a tooth, a footprint) | Surprising facts, "look closely" moments, tiny but important details |
| Close-up | A face or one object filling the frame | Emotion, reaction, a key object being introduced |
| Medium shot | Subject from about the waist up, or the whole small animal | The default for explaining what someone/something does |
| Wide shot | Whole subject plus its surroundings | Setting the scene, showing habitat, showing movement across space |
| Extreme wide / establishing | A huge environment, subject is small in it | Opening a new location or time period, showing how big or empty a place is |

## 2. Camera angles

| Angle | Effect | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Eye-level | Neutral, friendly, easy to read | Explanations, calm facts |
| Low angle (looking up) | Subject feels big, powerful, threatening | Huge animals, danger, authority, "towering" |
| High angle (looking down) | Subject feels small, weak, vulnerable | Small or helpless creatures, prey, being overwhelmed |
| Bird's-eye / top-down | Layout, patterns, scale comparison | Showing a group, a herd, a territory, objects laid out |
| Worm's-eye (from ground) | Extreme drama and size | A giant foot coming down, something looming overhead |
| Side-on profile | Clean, diagram-like, shows body shape | Anatomy, comparing two things side by side, walking/running |
| Over-the-shoulder / behind | Viewer shares the character's view | Approaching something, discovering something |
| Slightly tilted ("dutch") | Tension, chaos | Sudden danger, an escape, something going wrong |

## 3. Matching the shot to the line

Ask: *What should the viewer feel or notice in these few seconds?*

- **Scale or size** ("as tall as a building") -> low angle plus a tiny familiar object for comparison, or side-by-side in profile.
- **A surprising detail** ("its tongue was a meter long") -> close-up or extreme close-up on that detail.
- **A new place or time** ("Millions of years ago...") -> extreme wide establishing shot.
- **Danger or a hunt** -> low angle on the threat, or high angle on the prey, or a tilted wide shot.
- **A calm everyday behavior** ("it spent most of its day eating") -> eye-level medium shot.
- **Comparing things** -> side-on profile, both in one frame, similar vertical positions.
- **A list or pattern** -> bird's-eye with items evenly spaced.
- **An emotional beat or punchline** -> close-up on the face/reaction, with a "!" or sparkle doodle.

If a line is abstract, convert it into something drawable first (see the template reference), then choose the shot.

## 4. Sequence rules

- Don't use the same shot size or angle more than **two images in a row**.
- After a few close/medium shots, return to a **wide shot** so the viewer stays oriented.
- Alternate between busy and calm images. Two busy frames in a row feels tiring; two empty ones feels flat.
- When the location changes, start with a wide establishing shot of the new place.
- Keep the left-right direction consistent: if a character walks left-to-right in one image, don't flip it in the next image of the same scene unless the story says they turned around.
- The first image of the video should be the most striking one, since it's the thumbnail-adjacent moment.

## 5. Composition tips for flat-style images

- Flat images read best with **one clear subject** and a simple background (a color band for the ground, one color for the sky).
- Place the main subject **off-center** (rule of thirds) unless symmetry is the point. This leaves room for the editor's slow zoom or pan.
- State sizes as percentages of the frame ("fills about 60% of the width") and give the facing direction.
- Use **overlap** (a leaf in front of the animal, a rock in front of the water) to create depth, since there's no shading to do it.
- Use **scale cues**: put a small known object beside a giant one so the size is obvious without text.
- Keep the **horizon line** consistent within a location (for example, always the lower third) so cuts between images of the same place feel connected.
